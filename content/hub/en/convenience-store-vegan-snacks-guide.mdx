Korean convenience stores carry thousands of products, but almost none are labelled vegan. There is no mandatory vegan certification system in Korea, so identifying plant-based options requires reading the ingredient panel directly. For visitors unfamiliar with Korean, this guide covers which product types are worth checking, what non-vegan ingredients to look for, and how to scan a Korean label using a translation app.

## — Why Vegan Verification Is Harder Than It Looks

Products that appear plant-based often contain hidden animal-derived ingredients. A few common patterns worth knowing before you start shopping:

- **Natural flavour (천연향)**: May be derived from animal sources — the label does not specify
- **Whey or milk powder (유청, 분유)**: Frequently added to crackers, chips, and beverages in small quantities
- **Gelatin (젤라틴)**: Standard in most Korean jelly products; pork or beef derived unless otherwise stated
- **Bonito extract (가다랑어)**: Used in some flavoured snacks and seasoned products

The key Korean terms to scan for on any ingredient list: 우유 (milk), 유청 (whey), 분유 (milk powder), 달걀 (egg), 젤라틴 (gelatin), 가다랑어 (bonito), 멸치 (anchovy).

## — Snacks and Dry Foods Worth Checking

Some product types have shorter ingredient lists that are easier to verify on the spot.

**Nuts and dried fruit**

Individually portioned almonds, walnuts, and cashew mixes sold near the checkout counter tend to use minimal ingredients. Honey-coated versions fall outside strict vegan criteria — look for plain roasted options. Dried fruit mixes are straightforward once you confirm the coating and any additives.

**Plain rice crackers (쌀과자)**

Unseasoned or lightly salted rice crackers often contain only rice, starch, salt, and vegetable oil. Flavoured varieties — soy sauce, cheese, spicy — carry a higher chance of dairy or fish-based seasoning and take longer to verify.

**Roasted seaweed (김 스낵)**

Seaweed snacks with ingredients limited to seaweed, sesame oil, and salt are among the easiest to confirm. Some seasoned versions include bonito extract, so it is worth checking the ingredient list even when the product looks plain.

**Original-flavour potato chips**

The base version of most potato chip brands uses potato, vegetable oil, and salt — a short list. Honey butter, cheese, and cream-flavoured varieties contain dairy and are not worth checking further.

**Yakgwa (약과)**

This traditional Korean confection is made from wheat flour, sesame oil, ginger, and a sweetener. It does not typically contain dairy or egg in its standard form. Honey is the main ingredient that varies depending on individual vegan criteria.

## — Drinks, Jellies, and Sweet Snacks

Beverages are generally easier to evaluate than packaged snacks.

**Sikhye (식혜)**

Sikhye is a traditional Korean rice drink made from malted rice, water, and sugar. It does not contain dairy and is sold in cans and bottles across all three major convenience store chains. Check the ingredient list confirms rice and sugar as the main components.

**Fruit juice and carbonated drinks**

100% fruit juice, cider (clear carbonated soft drink), and cola-type beverages typically contain no animal-derived ingredients. One exception to watch for is carmine (카르민, E120), an insect-derived red colouring used in some fruit-flavoured drinks.

**Soy milk and plant-based drinks**

Soy milk (두유) is widely available in Korean convenience stores and is straightforward to verify. Almond-based drinks are also stocked at most GS25 and CU locations. These are easy options for protein without needing to navigate complex ingredient lists.

**Jelly products**

Jelly is the highest-risk category. Most Korean jelly products use animal-derived gelatin, and the ingredient panel will list 젤라틴 (gelatin) as a component. Products made with agar (한천) or pectin (펙틴) are plant-based alternatives, but they are less common. If the ingredient list is unclear or hard to read, skipping jelly entirely is the safer call.

## — How to Read the Ingredient Panel at the Store

Two practical methods for checking labels without Korean language skills.

**Translation app camera mode**

Google Translate's camera feature works directly on Korean ingredient panels — hold the camera over the back of the packaging and the app overlays a real-time translation. Naver Papago offers the same function and tends to handle Korean food terminology slightly better. Either app works well enough to identify the key terms listed above.

**A quick check sequence**

Rather than translating the full label, a faster approach:

1. Find the 원재료명 (ingredients) section on the back panel
2. Scan for 우유, 유청, 분유, 달걀 — dairy and egg
3. Scan for 젤라틴, 가다랑어, 멸치 — gelatin and fish
4. If 향료 (flavour/flavouring) appears, note whether it says 천연 (natural) — this is worth a second look

Products with five or fewer ingredients are the easiest to clear quickly. Long ingredient lists with multiple flavourings and seasonings take more time and carry more uncertainty.
